# Round 2: Debate

## Bezos

SEAT: BEZOS

I DISAGREE MOST WITH: CODIE. Her line: "ride the network, own the customer" by sitting between the buyer and Zipline or Wing. That is my own D case, so here is the "no buyer" side at full strength. Dispatch is not heavy lifting the operator can hand off. For Wing and Zipline it is the beer itself. "The undifferentiated heavy lifting by the way has to be done at World Class levels of Excellence or your vision will fail" (S012). Operators rent out what doesn't set them apart and keep what does. A layer that sits on their customer relationship is the first thing they build themselves. So D sold to operators has no buyer. My D case does not survive that.

I CONCEDE: Hormozi and Elon have it. Work backwards from the customer (S056). A hospital lab director does not want dispatch software. She wants the sample there in 20 minutes with chain of custody. She pays for the outcome. So I move to A as a managed service, running on software we write for ourselves.

ON THE CRUXES:
1. No dependable buyer for a middle layer sold to the big networks. The buyer is the health system, and it buys delivery.
2. Yes. Sell the outcome and subcontract the flying. A signed contract is a two-way door (S063). Margin is the risk: the subcontract costs $15 to $25 a run, so we need to charge more than that.
3. Retail D is worse. The networks sign up merchants directly, so the middle gets squeezed. The weakest part of my own case: I assumed the contacts are in healthcare.

STANCE: Conditional yes, A as a managed service

2. Within 90 days, 2 health systems sign paid pilots at $35 or more a run.

## Graham

SEAT: GRAHAM
I DISAGREE MOST WITH: Buffett. His case: if 3 LOIs don't come in 90 days, switch to E and keep the cash. My rebuttal: sitting on cash for 12 months teaches you nothing, and you are still default dead, just slower. "Launching too slowly has probably killed a hundred times more startups than launching too fast" (S178). If 90 days produce no LOIs, the market is telling you to change the buyer. It is not telling you to stop.

I CONCEDE: Bezos and Buffett on the buyer. Wing, Zipline and Amazon build their own software, so a middle layer is a component inside someone else's network. I also see that the hand-run version of D I proposed is a dispatcher booking flights for a customer. That is A sold as a managed service. Selling the outcome means you own the customer instead of being a component (S019). This moves me.

ON THE CRUXES:
1. For pure software, there are few buyers, maybe none. There is a buyer for the outcome.
2. Yes. Sell the delivery, run dispatch by hand, and build the software afterward. "I have never once seen a startup lured down a blind alley by trying too hard to make their initial users happy." (S096)
3. Retail at full strength: there are thousands of merchants, sales close in weeks, and the founder has warm intros. A merchant can't integrate with a drone network alone, and you could measure growth weekly. The problem is that the network decides which merchants it takes, and someone still pays $15 to $25 a drop. Retail beats medical only if the founder's contacts are really there, because a medical idea with no contacts is something you thought up. You didn't notice it (S098). Medical with real contacts beats both.

STANCE: Conditional yes
OPTION SCORES: A 7/10 · B 2/10 · C 4/10 · D 6/10 · E 2/10
PILLAR SCORE: 6/10
CONFIDENCE: 3/5
RUIN RISK: possible
MOVED: yes, A from 6 to 7 and D from 7 to 6. Run by hand, D becomes A, and selling the outcome owns the customer.
MY CONDITIONS FOR YES:
1. By day 30, the founder names 10 contacts in one industry who pay to move a specific item today.
2. By day 90, a certified operator agrees in writing to fly for us.
3. By day 120, one paid run has been delivered, dispatched by hand.

## Hormozi

SEAT: HORMOZI

I DISAGREE MOST WITH: Codie. Her argument is that we ride Zipline's and Wing's networks and own the customer. My answer: in that setup we own nothing. Wing already sells to Walmart directly. A merchant who can plug into Wing has no reason to pay a middleman, so our price gets compared to free. That is a bad-offer symptom (Grand Slam Offer, S114).

I CONCEDE: Bezos's point that health systems take 9 to 12 months to buy. That hurts my D card too, so my first sale has to replace a budget they already spend, like a ground courier contract, instead of opening a new line item.

ON THE CRUXES:
1. Operators won't pay for this layer. The big three build their own, and the small ones are broke. Health systems don't buy dispatch software, they buy samples arriving on time. So the software layer has no real buyer. "If you want to win at software, you have to commit to making no money for basically seven years. Zero." (S034)
2. Sell the outcome. My math, with assumed numbers: we charge $60 a run, the operator gets $25 and we keep $35. At 20 runs a day that is about $21k a month of gross profit per hospital. Software at $3k a month and 80% margin is $2.4k, so the outcome is worth about 9 times more per customer. Done for you cuts effort to zero (Value Equation, S115). The software becomes our internal tool, not the product.
3. No. Retail is low-ticket, and the networks already own the merchant. If the founder's contacts are only in retail, I'd go to E, not retail D.

STANCE: Conditional yes, on A sold as a managed service.
OPTION SCORES: A 8/10 · B 1/10 · C 4/10 · D 5/10 · E 3/10
PILLAR SCORE: 8/10
CONFIDENCE: 3/5
RUIN RISK: possible. We carry the liability if a sample is lost.
MOVED: yes, from D to A as a managed service, because health systems pay for outcomes, not tools.

MY CONDITIONS FOR YES:
1. A certified operator signs a subcontract at $25 or less a run within 60 days.
2. One health system signs a paid pilot at $50 or more a run within 90 days.
3. In the pilot, the first 30 days of cash are more than 2 times what it cost to win and serve that customer (S037).

## Buffett

SEAT: BUFFETT

I DISAGREE MOST WITH: Codie. Her argument is "ride Zipline's and Wing's networks and own the customer." My rebuttal is that whoever owns the bridge sets the toll. If our product only works through their API, they can raise the price, copy the feature or close the gate, and a company that just raised $600M has every reason to do it. That's renting space in someone else's castle, not owning a moat. "Every business that we look at we think of as an economic castle. And castles are subject to marauders." (S005)

I CONCEDE: Hormozi and Elon have the stronger point. The health system holds the checkbook and pays a premium for the delivery itself. My D case rested on a software buyer I never named, and that moves A up.

ON THE CRUXES:
1. The "no buyer" side wins. Wing, Zipline and Amazon write their own code. The smaller operators are few and short of cash, so each one would be 20% or more of our revenue. Dispatch software they could build in a quarter is a commodity: "In a business selling a commodity-type product, it's impossible to be a lot smarter than your dumbest competitor." (S063) D as software sold to operators doesn't survive. D as the health system's own tool does.
2. Yes, sell the outcome. Charge per run with a monthly minimum, pay the operator per run and keep the difference. The software is our own tool. The catch is liability: one lost sample is a front-page story. "We can afford to lose money but we can't afford to lose reputation." (S028)
3. No. Retail trips lose about $10 each, and Walmart already owns the merchant. Nobody pays a software fee on a trip that loses money. If the contacts are in retail, that points to E, not to D for retail.

STANCE: Conditional yes, on A run as a managed service.

3. No. Retail merchant integration is the exact layer the networks need for their own utilization, so they will build it. If the contacts are in retail, I score that version 4.

## Chair notes

**Who moved.** Five of six seats moved. Bezos, Hormozi, Buffett and Elon moved their top pick from D to A run as a managed service. Graham moved A up and D down and now ranks A first. Codie lowered D but still ranks it first, for the retail merchant version.

**Crux 1 (is there a software buyer?): resolved, no.** Bezos and Buffett argued the "no buyer" side and then conceded it themselves. No seat named an operator that would pay. The surviving form of D is software the startup uses internally.

**Crux 2 (sell the outcome?): resolved, yes.** Hormozi's math carried it: about $35 of gross profit per run against about $2.4k a month for a software seat (assumed numbers). Elon's point that software cannot shorten a 12 to 24 month approval wait backed it.

**Crux 3 (retail versus medical): not resolved.** Graham and Codie say retail wins only if the founder's contacts are really in retail. Hormozi, Buffett and Elon say retail contacts point to E, not to retail software. Every seat flagged that the brief does not say where the contacts are. This is the open question.

**Open numbers.** Operator cost per run ranges from $20 (Bezos) to $25 (Hormozi). Price per run ranges from $35 (Bezos) to $50 (Hormozi). The resolution uses Hormozi's figures because they come with the worked math.
