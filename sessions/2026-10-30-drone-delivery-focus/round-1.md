# Round 1: Opening positions

## Bezos

SEAT: BEZOS (Strategy and decisions)
STANCE: Conditional yes
OPTION SCORES: A 6/10 · B 2/10 · C 5/10 · D 7/10 · E 3/10
PILLAR SCORE: 8/10
CONFIDENCE: 3/5
RUIN RISK: possible
DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: Do D pointed at A. Build dispatch and order-integration software for health systems that want lab and pharmacy runs, and let an operator that already holds a certificate fly them.

WHY:
1. Sort the door first (Type 1 and Type 2, S063). For a company with $750k and 2 to 3 people, buying aircraft and chasing a Part 135 certificate is a one-way door. A new area can take 12 to 24 months to approve, and at an assumed $35k a month in burn that is the whole runway. Software sold to a health system is a two-way door. If it misses, you change the customer and keep the code.
2. Price of admission (S035): "The price of admission is too high." The drone and airspace layer is being built by companies with $600M rounds. A founder with no aviation background should ride that layer. The founder's real assets are software skill and industry contacts, and the work no operator wants to do is the ordering, the chain of custody and the compliance inside a hospital.
3. Don't fight the giants for one prize. B lines up against Wing, Walmart, Zipline and Amazon at $15 to $25 a delivery against an $8 to $12 target. "I don't like winner-take-all industries because your odds of winning in a winner-take-all industry is low." (S013). E waits for Part 108 while the runway runs down, and "rapid change favors small, dynamic, nimble startup companies." (S023). That rule is the change, so you want to be ready the day it lands.

THE RISK I SEE: There are too few buyers. Operators build their own software, and health systems take 9 to 12 months to buy (my assumption), so you get no paying customer inside the 12-month goal.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: If, out of 10 conversations, 2 health-system logistics leads will sign a paid pilot letter within 90 days, I go to 9/10. If none will, the founder's contacts are not where I'm assuming (I'm assuming they're in healthcare), and C becomes the variant.

WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Book 10 calls with hospital lab and pharmacy logistics heads, plus 1 with an operator that could fly the runs, and ask each to name what they would pay for.

QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: Which industry are your contacts actually in, and who among them would sign a paid contract this quarter?

## Graham

SEAT: GRAHAM (Product and startups)
STANCE: Conditional yes
OPTION SCORES: A 6/10 · B 2/10 · C 5/10 · D 7/10 · E 2/10
PILLAR SCORE: 6/10
CONFIDENCE: 3/5
RUIN RISK: possible
DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: D, but narrowed hard. Build the software the founder's own industry contacts need to buy drone delivery from operators who already fly, and sell it to one buyer type. Medical is my guess for that buyer.

WHY:
1. Small intense fire (S010, S174). "A need that's narrow but genuine is a better starting point than one that's broad but hypothetical." (S174) The founder's real edge is contacts plus code. That points to a buyer they can name, not to aircraft. If they can't name 20 lab or pharmacy managers who would be bummed if this went away, they don't have a market yet.
2. Superlinear returns (S035). Wing is targeting 60M households, and Zipline raised $600M. "If your product is only half as good as your competitor's, you don't get half as many customers. You get no customers, and you go out of business." (S035) In B, $750k buys no drones that matter. Each delivery costs $15 to $25 against the $8 to $12 the margins need, and each drone does only 3 to 8 a day. B is the lean, and I'd kill it.
3. Launch fast (S178). "Launching too slowly has probably killed a hundred times more startups than launching too fast" (S178) New areas take 12 to 24 months to approve. If A, B, or C means flying your own aircraft, that approval wait uses up most of the 18 to 24 months of runway before the first user. E is the same slow launch, on purpose. With D, a hand-run version can be in front of a customer in weeks.

THE RISK I SEE: The real buyer of D turns out to be big operators who build their own software. Then this becomes a partnership play, and "Partnerships too usually don't work." (S096)

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: The founder has 3 or more health systems ready to sign paid medical runs, plus an existing Part 135 operator willing to fly them under its certificate within 6 months. Then A, done by hand on someone else's aircraft, beats D.

WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Call 15 contacts in the target industry. Ask what they would pay this month to move one specific item faster. Do the dispatch by hand in a spreadsheet for the first one who says yes.

QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: Which named person in your network is losing money every week because a sample or part arrives late, and what do they pay to move it today?

## Hormozi

SEAT: HORMOZI (Offers, pricing, and sales)
STANCE: Conditional yes
OPTION SCORES: A 6/10 · B 2/10 · C 4/10 · D 7/10 · E 3/10
PILLAR SCORE: 7/10
CONFIDENCE: 3/5
RUIN RISK: none
DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: I'd do D with a narrow focus: dispatch, compliance and order-integration software sold to health systems and the operators who fly for them, with a paid setup fee on every contract.

WHY:
1. B fails the math before it fails the market. A drop costs $15 to $25 today. Say a consumer pays $5 (my assumption). You lose $10 to $20 a drop, and at 5 drops per drone per day that is $50 to $100 lost per drone per day. You are running a negative gross margin against a $600M-funded Zipline. You can't compute LTV to CAC on a loss. Kill the lean. (LTV to CAC, S070)
2. D is the only option that can bill inside 12 months. My assumed numbers: $3k a month per site at 80% gross margin with a 24-month life gives $57.6k lifetime gross profit. A founder selling through warm intros might spend about $10k fully loaded to land a contract. That is about 5.8 to 1, right at the 6 to 1 floor for a sale that people staff (S089). Add a $15k setup fee. "30-day cash must be greater than two times the cost of acquiring customer and the cost of fulfilling that customer." (S037) Your customers pay for the next customer, so the $750k doesn't have to.
3. A has the price, and D sells into it. Health systems pay for fast, certain delivery and less effort (Value Equation, S115). The 12 to 24 months of Part 135 approvals kill A as an operator. They don't kill it as a buyer of software. One product, one avatar, one channel (S059). E waits on rules nobody controls and earns $0.

THE RISK I SEE: The buyer pool is tiny. If five operators fly everything and build their own software, you have no market. "If you want to win at software, you have to commit to making no money for basically seven years. Zero." (S034)

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: If fewer than 3 of 20 health system or operator conversations agree to pay a $15k setup fee for a pilot, then D is a hobby and A or E moves up.

WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Contact 100 people from the founder's network and book 10 calls with a paid-pilot offer.

QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: Which industry are your contacts actually in, and will one of them sign a paid pilot in the next 30 days?

## Buffett

SEAT: BUFFETT (Money and capital)
STANCE: Conditional yes
OPTION SCORES: A 4/10 · B 1/10 · C 4/10 · D 7/10 · E 5/10
PILLAR SCORE: 7/10
CONFIDENCE: 3/5
RUIN RISK: none
DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: Go with D, software that operators and health systems buy, with the first product aimed at the industry where the founder already has contacts. Buy no aircraft.

WHY:
1. B fails the cash math. Each delivery costs $15 to $25 and needs to get to $8 to $12, so every trip loses about $10. Our $750k comes to roughly $36k a month over 21 months (my assumption), and we'd be spending it against Zipline's $600M raise and Walmart's 1 million deliveries. "The worst sort of business is one that grows rapidly, requires significant capital to engender the growth, and then earns little or no money." (Great, good, and gruesome, S080)
2. The founder knows contacts and software. He doesn't know aviation. "The important thing is not how big the circle is the important thing is staying inside the circle." (Circle of competence, S022) Options A and C put us in the aircraft business, with a Part 135 certificate and 12 to 24 months of approvals. That would use up most of our runway before we earn a dollar (my assumption).
3. D has the best capital efficiency. A dollar of software revenue needs no hangar and no pilot, and when Part 108 lands it brings more operators who all need dispatch and compliance tools. "only a very low-cost operator or someone operating in a protected, and usually small, niche can sustain high profitability levels." (Franchise versus commodity, S060) E keeps the cash but gives up 12 months of learning, and it doesn't hit the success test. "Though the early bird gets the worm, the second mouse gets the cheese." (S088) We can be the second mouse and still be building.

I'll admit the Berkshire rules misfire on a seed-stage company. That's why this is conditional and not a strong yes.

THE RISK I SEE: The buyers are few, and they build in-house. Wing, Zipline and Amazon write their own software, so we could end up with one customer who can pull the plug.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: If the founder can't get 3 signed letters of intent at $2k or more a month within 90 days, I'd switch to E and keep the cash.

WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Call ten contacts and ask each one what they would pay this quarter for dispatch or compliance software.

QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: Name the three customers who aren't Wing, Zipline or Amazon who would pay you in the next 12 months, and say what each would pay.

## Chair notes

**Unanimity warning.** All six seats picked D, scoring it 7 to 8, within 1 point. Under the scoring rules that is a warning, not a win. Every seat also scored the founder's lean, B, at 1 or 2.

**What nobody argued.** Every seat named the same risk for D: the few operators that matter (Wing, Zipline, Amazon) build their own software, so the middle layer may have no buyers. No seat tested it. Every seat also assumed the founder's contacts are in healthcare, which the brief does not say.

**Cruxes for Round 2**
1. Is there a real paying buyer for a software layer between customers and operators, or do Wing, Zipline and Amazon own that layer? All seats raised it; none answered it. Bezos and Buffett must argue the "no buyer" side at full strength.
2. Should the startup sell the delivery outcome, not software? The strongest unargued alternative is A as a managed service: the founder sells medical delivery to health systems and subcontracts the flying to a certified operator. Hormozi and Elon must argue it at full strength.
3. If the founder's contacts are in retail and food (the lean), does D aimed at merchants plugging into existing drone networks beat D aimed at medical? Graham and Codie must argue the retail version at full strength.
